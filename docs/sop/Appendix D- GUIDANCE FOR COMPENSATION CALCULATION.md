#  Compensation Determination under RFCTARR Act, 2013

The determination of compensation is governed under Sections 26 to Section 30 of the RFCTLARR Act, 2013 (along with Schedule I).

The compensation determination stages and an illustration is presented below in Tables I and II. There are varying practices adopted by different state governments in arriving at the land compensation award, while broadly following the prescribed norms. The differences exist in the quantum on which escalation/solatium is paid, discounting/non-discounting of outlier transactions and even in the calculation of average.

Given these varied practices, the guiding factor for calculation would be the spirit of the amended legislation and the objective of the changed compensation determination process, which is to help arrive at a 'fair compensation' for the lands proposed for acquisition. Also, existing case laws provide good insights for interpretation for some of the provisions. While determining compensation, these may be given adequate attention. The case laws dealing with important provisions are presented in the Notes below:

##  Important Notes:

Before enactment of RFCATLARR ACT 2013 compensation under CBA Act, 1957 used to be prepared as under:

1. Market value of the land as on the date of Notification u/s 4(1) of CBA (A&D) Act, 1957 along with damages, if any, as provided in section 13 (5) of the said Act.

2. Solatium 30% of the market value of land.

3. Additional Payment 12% per annum of the market value of land for the period from the date of Notification u/s 4(1) till the date of Notification u/s 9(1) of CBA (A&D) Act, 1957.

4. Interest 9% of the market value for one year from the date of Notification u/s 9(1).

5. Interest   15% per annum of the market value for the period thereafter till payment is made

However, when compensation is settled after the cut-off date ($1^{st}$ September 2015), it is determined with the impact of first and second schedule of RFCATLARR ACT 2013. There is dispute regarding payment of enhancement under section 30(3). As per the said provision enhancement @ 12% is to be provided from the date of publication of SIA. Generally, SIA is not done during acquisition of land under CBA (A&D) Act. So, huge confusion has been created regarding the period for which enhancement of market value of land is to be provided. To get away with the problem following alternative method has been resorted to:

1. Compensation is calculated based on market value of land which is applicable on the date of determination/ award of compensation instead of market value on the date of notification u/s 4 of CBA (A&D) Act.

2. No enhancement is added as compensation is based on current market price of land.

1. Circle Rates: The market value that may have been specified/ determined under the Indian Stamp Act, 1899 for the purposes of registration of property transactions. It is the value of land that the competent authority in a state may notify as a "guideline" rate for different jurisdictions from time to time for the purpose of registration of property transactions and the value of stamp duty payable thereon.

"The guideline market value can be a relevant piece of evidence only if they are assessed by statutorily appointed Expert Committees, in accordance with the prescribed assessment procedure (either street-wise, or road-wise, or area-wise, or village-wise) and finalised after inviting objections and published in the Gazette (SC-2009: Lal Chand Vs. Union of India and Anr).

Given the above, the Collector/ Circle Rates for the villages under acquisition for a period of previous 3-4 years may be assessed for any extraordinary surge and if yes, the reason for such a sudden increase may be ascertained.

2. Category of Land: The category of land (agricultural/ residential/ industrial/commercial) has to be ascertained on the date of notification which is the cut-off date for determination of market value. It is important to take note of the following:

● The category of land does not necessarily change from "agricultural" to 'non-agricultural' only because the landowner has put his land to such "non-agricultural" use, unless he has got the land use changed with the approval of the competent authority and paid the prescribed charges for the same as per the state statutes/rules.

● However, the Courts have always recognized the potential to which the land is reasonably capable of being used in future in assessing compensation.

The potential to which the land is reasonably capable of being used in future by the owner should be taken into account in assessing compensation (SC-2011: Bilkis and Ors. vs. State of Maharashtra and Ors)

The amount of compensation cannot be ascertained with mathematical accuracy. A comparable instance has to be identified having regard to the proximity from time angle as well as proximity from situation angle (SC-2005: Viluben Jhalejar Contractor (D) by Lrs. vs. State of Gujarat

Even if a land is not a non-agricultural land, if the same is surrounded by non-agricultural lands and the same is likely to be converted into non-agricultural land as per the say of the Land Acquisition Officer himself, it is certainly an important and relevant factor for the purpose of fixing the rate at which the compensation is to be awarded at par or with close proximity with the rate in respect of non-agricultural land. For the afore-mentioned purpose, this Court must keep in mind that the distance of the lands sought to be acquired apart from other factors from the highway also plays an important role. So far as the agricultural lands are concerned, even if they had the potentiality of being converted into a non-agricultural lands as on the date of notification, they were agricultural lands albeit in a developed area. The valuation thereof may be determined at 50 percent of the developed land (SC-2009-Dy. Collector, Land Acquisition, Gujarat & Ors. vs. Madhubai Gobarbhai).

The category of land has to be ascertained on the date of notification which is the cut-off date. However, since the revenue records may not be updated at all times, the nature of land shown on the cut-off date may undergo a change subject to production of satisfactory evidence by the landowners. It is important to judically consider the sales data before excluding the same from average calculation. It has to be ensured that the excluded transactions are not only skewed but also not indicative of the prevalent market value. In other words, higher transaction value owing to the location factor may not be excluded outright. Also, skewed values may be considered, both for higher and lower values for the exclusion from the average calculation. The underlying objective of the compensation determination exercise is to arrive at the "fair compensation", a price at which a willing buyer would have purchase from a willing seller.

3. Similarity of Land: Comparative sales method has been the most well recognized method for land acquisition compensation determination by Courts. Under this method the Land Acquisition Officer has to enquire about transaction of sale of similar lands. The market value of the land is to be assessed on the basis of sale price of other comparable lands in the adjoining area. As regarding the factors to be considered for sales, the following is considered by the Courts.

#  Factors to be considered for Sales

(1) Sale must be a genuine transaction,

(2) Sale deed must have been executed at the time proimate to the date of issue of notification under Section 4 of the Act,

(3) Sale must be in the vicinity of the acquired land,

(4) Sales must be similar to the acquired land, and

(5) Size of plot of the land covered by the sales be comparable to the land acquired.

However, if there is a dissimilarity in regard to locality, shape, site or nature of land between land covered by sales and land acquired, it is open to the court to proportionately reduce the compensation for acquired land than what is reflected in the sales depending upon the disadvantages attached with the acquired land (SC-2011: Valliyammal and Ors. vs. Special Tahsildar, Land Acquisition).

The transactions may be considered for the village for which land is being acquired. In the absence of transactions or limited transactions, sale transactions of nearby villages may also be considered. However, as provided in Section 26(3); if acquisition of land is proposed in areas where transactions in land are restricted by law and registered transactions are not available or the market value has not been specified under the Indian Stamp Act, 1899, it is only the state government (not appropriate government or Collector) that can fix the minimum floor price

4. Small Extents of Land and Deduction: To consider sale transaction of a smaller property for fixing the market value of a large property, a deduction is made taking into consideration the expenses required for development of the larger tract to make smaller plots within the area. However, if there is no evidence of sale of large pieces of land or area comparable with the acquired land is available, the sale transactions of smaller pieces of land can be taken into consideration and a reasonable cut can be imposed in the market price as represented by them so as to arrive at the correct market value of the acquired land.

It is now settled by series of judgments of this Court that determination of the compensation on sq. ft. basis is a wrong principle of law, particularly when large extents of lands are sought to be acquired for public purpose. The question is: whether a normal, ordinary and prudent man, in given circumstances, would be willing to purchase 1 acre and 6 gunthas of land when offered in normal market conditions on sq. ft. basis? If the property is situated in well developed cities like Nariman Point in Bombay and Connaught Place in Delhi or other similar industrially well developed areas, the offer for sale on sq. ft. basis may be understandable. One would, as a prudent purchaser, shudder to purchase lands on sq. ft. basis (SC-1996: Special Land Acquisition Officer, Bangalore vs. V.T. Velu )

While fixing the market rate, very often, documents of smaller extent would be taken as the basis. The normal rule in fixing compensation for large extent of land with reference to the value shown in the sale document of lesser extent is that there must be suitable deduction (SC-2011: Valliyammal and Ors. vs. Special Tahsildar, Land Acquisition)

5. Rate Fixation Across Lands: Is it appropriate to fix uniform rate for entire land acquired? The Apex Court has held that this depends upon the extent of the land acquired, the location, proximity to an access road/Main Road/Highway or to a City/Town/Village, and other relevant circumstances:

(A). Small & compact extent of land and entire area is similarly situated: Appropriate to value the acquired land at a single uniform rate.

(B). Large tract of land with some lands facing a main road/national highway and other lands being in the interior, value the lands adjacent to the main road at a higher rate

(C) Large tract of land on the outskirts of a town (one end of the acquired lands adjoining the town boundary, the other end being two to three kilo metres away): Belting method and the belt or strip adjacent to the town boundary will be given the highest price, the remotest belt will be awarded the lowest rate, the belts/strips of lands falling in between, will be awarded gradually reducing rates from the highest to the lowest.

(D) Large tract of land with a radius of one to two kilometres but the entire land acquired is far away from any town or city limits, without any special Main road access: Logical to award the entire land, one uniform rate. The fact that the distance between one point to another point in the acquired lands, may be as much as two to three kilometres may not make any difference.
